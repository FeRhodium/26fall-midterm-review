• Initially proposed for distributed caching.
• Every entry in a counting Bloom filter is a small counter (rather than a
  single bit).
    • When an item is inserted into the set, the corresponding counters are
      each incremented by 1.
    • When an item is deleted from the set, the corresponding counters are
      each decremented by 1.
• To avoid counter overflow, its size must be sufficiently large. It was found
  in practice that 4 bits per counter are enough.

• On a cache miss a proxy server needs to determine if another proxy
  cache holds the desired Web page (instead of trying to obtain the page

• Proxies must know the content of other proxy caches.
• They send periodically Bloom filters that represent the content of their

• Each cache maintains a counting Bloom filter (because of dynamic
  content), broadcasts periodically a simple Bloom filter to other proxies.

Fair queue management with a counting Bloom filter



                                                                                         We don’t want to discard a
                                                                                          packet at random. Why?

• Setting: many flows share one router queue.
• Problem: when the queue is full and a packet arrives, whose packets do we discard?
• Goal: penalize flows that “contribute most to congestion”: those that keep many packets in
  the queue for a long time.
• Constraint: millions of flows, little memory: no per-flow state.
• Technique: mark packets with a probability that grows while their flow keeps the queue
  highly loaded; discard marked packets with priority.
• Tool: a counting Bloom filter. CSC4120 Design and Analysis of Algorithms - C. Courcoubetis                          16

What we learned
• A Bloom filter can hold a policy, not just a set. Counters and marking probabilities
  in 𝑚 bins enforce fairness among millions of flows, with no per-flow state. The cost
  per packet is 𝑘 hash operations and 𝑘 counter accesses, and the answers are
  approximate.
• The structure is a feedback loop. Marking slows down a heavy flow (if it reacts to
  losses, as TCP does), its bins drain, and 𝑝(𝑖) comes back down. A flow that ignores
  marking keeps pushing 𝑝(𝑖) up to the cap of 1. The scheme adapts to how senders
  behave, without knowing their rates.
• Errors are cheap here. A collision can only make an innocent flow look guilty if all
  of its 𝑘 bins are congested at once, which is rare. Even then the cost is an
  occasional extra discard, not a wrong answer. That is why an approximate structure
  with collisions is good enough, as in many Bloom filter applications.
